01

What is a pedigree, and why bother?

A pedigree is a diagram of a family tree that tracks how a specific trait — a disorder, a blood type, a physical feature — has passed from generation to generation. Instead of listing family members, it uses standardized shapes and lines so that anyone trained to read it can immediately see who is related to whom, who shows the trait, and who married into the family.

Biologists and genetic counselors use real pedigrees to figure out how a trait is inherited (dominant? recessive? carried on the X chromosome?) and to calculate the probability that a future child will inherit it. You'll do exactly that in this guide: build a pedigree from a written family history, then work backward from a finished pedigree to deduce genotypes that are never directly stated.

Generations

Rows are numbered with Roman numerals (I, II, III…), oldest at the top. Everyone in one row is the same generation.

Individuals

Each person gets an Arabic number within their row, left to right, e.g. individual II-3.

Lines

A horizontal line joins two mates. A vertical line drops from that union to their children.

02

The symbol key

Every pedigree in this guide — and almost every one you'll ever see — uses this same set of shapes. Shape encodes sex; fill encodes phenotype.

Some textbooks add a half-filled shape with a dot in the middle for a known heterozygous carrier who doesn't show the trait. It isn't in this worksheet's key, so we'll write carrier genotypes out in words instead — but you may see that symbol elsewhere.

03

Draw one, step by step

The Thomas family, described in words: "Tom and Diane have three children — Anna, Mary, and Teddy, oldest to youngest. Anna is married with two children, Will and Vanessa. Mary is married with one child, Patrick. Teddy is unmarried. Everyone is right-handed except Tom, Anna, and Vanessa. Right-handedness is dominant."

The five moves you just made, every time:
  1. Place a generation's couple, oldest generation first, and join them with a horizontal line.
  2. Drop a single line from the middle of that marriage line, then branch it into a horizontal "sibling line" above the children, oldest child on the left.
  3. Give every child their own vertical stem down from the sibling line.
  4. Fill in shapes for anyone who shows the trait; leave everyone else outlined.
  5. Repeat for each spouse who marries into the family and each new generation, then label rows I, II, III… and number each person left to right.

05

Five rules for pinning down a genotype

A pedigree only ever shows you phenotypes (filled or not). Genotype is something you deduce — sometimes with total certainty, sometimes not at all.

  1. 1

    An affected individual with a recessive trait is homozygous recessive. There's no other way to show a recessive phenotype, so aa is certain the moment you see a filled shape.

  2. 2

    Two unaffected parents with an affected (recessive) child are both obligate carriers. Each parent must have donated a recessive allele, so both are heterozygous — even though neither shows the trait.

  3. 3

    Any child of a homozygous-recessive parent must carry at least one recessive allele. If that child is unaffected, the other allele — from the other parent — must be dominant, which makes the child's genotype heterozygous for certain, not just probable.

  4. 4

    On the X chromosome, sons get their only X from their mother, and daughters get one X from each parent. An affected father can never pass an X-linked trait to his sons (they get his Y), but he always passes his X — and whatever's on it — to every daughter.

  5. 5

    Some genotypes simply can't be pinned down from the pedigree given. When neither a person's parents nor their children give it away, the honest answer is "heterozygous or homozygous dominant — can't tell," written as A_. That's not a wrong answer; it's the correct one.

Worked pedigrees

Autosomal recessive

Warm-up: blue eyes

Blue eyes (bb) is recessive to brown eyes (B_). Generation I is the grandparents; Generation II is their children plus a son-in-law; Generation III is one grandchild.

Notice: I-1 is unfilled (brown eyes) but must be Bb — his daughter II-2 is bb, so he had to have contributed a b. You determined a "hidden" genotype from a child two branches away.

Autosomal recessive

Pedigree 1 — Albinism

Albinism is caused by a recessive allele: use D for the normal-pigment allele and d for albinism.

If individuals II-1 and II-2 had another child, what's the chance it will have normal pigment?

II-1 = Dd (he must carry d — his daughter III-1 is dd, and the only way she gets a d from her father is if he carries one). II-2 = dd (affected, so obligate).

Cross Dd × dd:

Dd
dDddd
dDddd

Answer: 1/2 (50%) chance the next child has normal pigmentation, and 1/2 (50%) chance of albinism.

X-linked recessive

Pedigree 2 — Hemophilia

Hemophilia is caused by a recessive allele on the X chromosome (Xh). Normal clotting is XH. A hemophiliac female would need to be XhXh; a carrier female is XHXh and clots normally.

If III-2 (the hemophiliac son) marries a woman who is XHXH, what's the chance their first child has hemophilia?

Cross XhY × XHXH:

XhY
XHXHXhXHY
XHXHXhXHY

Answer: 0%. Every daughter becomes an unaffected carrier (XHXh) and every son is unaffected (XHY) — none of their children can have hemophilia, though every daughter silently carries the allele forward.

X-linked dominant

Pedigree 3 — Vitamin D–resistant rickets

This form of rickets is caused by a dominant allele on the X chromosome (XR); normal is recessive Xr. Dominant X-linked traits give a distinctive pattern — watch what happens to the sons of an affected father.

If I-1 had the same phenotype as his wife (affected, XRY) instead of being unaffected, what fraction of their children would have rickets?

Cross XRY × XRXr:

XRXr
XRXRXRXRXr
YXRYXrY

All daughters (100%) are affected — they always receive their father's only X, which carries XR. Half of sons (50%) are affected, depending on which X they receive from mom. Averaged across all children: 3 out of 4 (75%) will have rickets.

Autosomal dominant

Pedigree 4 — PTC tasting

The ability to taste the bitter chemical PTC is dominant (A); nontasting is recessive (aa). Two separate families merge here through a marriage in Generation II.

If I-4 (right-hand grandmother) had instead married III-4 (her own great-grandson-in-law's generation — a nontaster), what's the probability each of their children is a taster?

I-4 must be Aa — her daughter II-4 is a nontaster (aa), so I-4 had to contribute an a even though she tastes PTC herself. III-4 is a nontaster, so he's aa.

Cross Aa × aa:

Aa
aAaaa
aAaaa

Answer: 1/2 (50%) chance each child is a taster.

07

Practice scenarios

These start from a written description, not a finished diagram — sketch your own pedigree first, then check it against the one below.

Autosomal recessive · carrier probability

Scenario 1 — Cystic fibrosis

A woman's sister has cystic fibrosis (recessive, cc). Neither of their parents has the disease. What's the chance the mother is a carrier? What's the chance the woman herself is a carrier?

Chance the mother is a carrier: 100%, not a probability at all. Both parents are phenotypically normal, yet their daughter is cc. The only way an unaffected parent contributes a c allele is by being a heterozygous carrier (Cc) — so both parents are obligate carriers, guaranteed.

Chance the woman is a carrier: 2/3. Cross Cc × Cc gives offspring in a 1 CC : 2 Cc : 1 cc ratio. We're told the woman is unaffected, so we drop the cc outcome and ask only among the remaining 3 possibilities: 2 of those 3 are Cc. That gives 2/3 — noticeably higher than the "default" 1/2 you might have guessed, because we've already used the information that she's unaffected.

Autosomal dominant · late onset

Scenario 2 — Huntington's disease

Huntington's disease is caused by a dominant allele but its symptoms don't appear until around age 40. John's father has just begun showing symptoms. What's the chance John will develop the disease?

Huntington's is rare enough that an affected person is assumed heterozygous (Hh) rather than homozygous. John's mother is unaffected (hh).

Cross Hh × hh → 1/2 Hh (will eventually develop the disease) : 1/2 hh (won't).

Answer: 50%. Late onset doesn't change the genetics — it just means John (and everyone he might have children with) may carry the allele for decades without knowing it. This is exactly why dominant, late-onset conditions raise such difficult questions about genetic testing.

Multiple alleles · codominance

Scenario 3 — ABO blood type

Blood type uses three alleles: IA and IB are codominant with each other, and both are dominant over i. Two individuals in this family tree (shown with a ?) were never typed directly — work out their blood group from their children.

I-2 must be type A (IAi). Her husband I-1 is type O (ii) and can only ever pass an i. Their son II-3 is type O, so I-2 must also carry an i. Their daughter II-2 is type A, and since dad has no IA to give, that allele had to come from mom — so I-2 is IAi.

The marry-in husband of II-2 must be type B (IBi). Their children are III-1 (type B) and III-2 (type O). II-2 can only pass IA or i, never IB — so the IB in III-1 must be his, and the i in III-2 must also be his (his own genotype is therefore fully pinned down as IBi, even though he was never typed).

One individual (II-1) can't be fully determined. She's a child of a type-O father and a type-A (IAi) mother with no children of her own shown, so she could be type O (ii) or type A (IAi) — the pedigree simply doesn't say, and that's a legitimate final answer.
